Air Fryer Potatoes (Times and Temperatures by Cut)

Air fryer potatoes by cut: wedges, cubes, baby potatoes, slices, hash browns and sweet potatoes, with temperatures, times and the soak-and-dry rule.

Potatoes are the highest-volume air fryer question there is. The difference between a crisp wedge and a pale, limp one is not the machine — it is starch, water and space in the basket.

Times vary by appliance model — use a thermometer for meat and your eyes for potatoes. Wattage, basket size and how full the basket is will move every number below.

The starch rule: soak, then dry completely#

Cut potatoes release starch, and that starch is glue. It makes pieces stick together, steams the surfaces that touch, and leaves clumps that are crisp on one side and soft on the other. A 10–30 minute soak in cold water rinses that surface starch off.

Drying matters more. Water on the surface has to evaporate before the potato can brown, so a wet potato steams first and crisps late. Drain, spread the pieces on a towel, and pat them dry.

How much oil#

About 1 tablespoon of oil per pound of potatoes — roughly 15 mL per 450 g — and no more than 2 tablespoons for a full 2-pound basket. Toss until every piece has a faint sheen.

More oil does not make crispier potatoes, just greasier ones, and the excess pools in the basket where it burns and smokes. Use a neutral oil with a high smoke point, and finish with butter or olive oil after the basket comes out.

Why parboiling helps#

A raw potato is dense. By the time the middle is cooked through, the surface has been in 400°F / 205°C air long enough to dry out and turn leathery. Parboiling for 5–8 minutes in salted water cooks the inside partway before the basket sees it, so the air fryer only has to finish the job and crisp the surface.

Parboiled surfaces also turn rough and chalky as starch granules loosen, and a rough surface crisps faster than a smooth one. Drain well and toss them in oil while hot.

The single-layer rule#

An air fryer is a small convection oven. Anything you pile up blocks the air, and the food underneath steams instead of crisping. One layer, with space between pieces, matters for potatoes more than for any other food.

If the batch does not fit in one layer, cook it in two batches. Two batches at 20 minutes beat one crowded batch at 35.

Five mistakes with air fryer potatoes#

  1. Wet potatoes. Water turns to steam before the surface can brown. Dry them every time.
  2. Too much oil. Over 2 tablespoons per 2 pounds and the basket smokes.
  3. A crowded basket. More than one layer means steamed potatoes with a few crisp edges.
  4. Uneven cuts. A 1/2-inch piece and a 1-inch piece cannot finish together.
  5. Salting thin cuts too early. Salt draws water out of slices and shredded hash browns.

Frequently asked questions#

What temperature do I cook potatoes in an air fryer?

Most cuts do well at 390°F / 199°C to 400°F / 205°C. Sweet potatoes are the exception: keep wedges at 380°F / 193°C.

Do I have to soak potatoes before air frying?

No, but it helps: a 10–30 minute soak rinses off surface starch, so the pieces stay separate and crisp. If you skip it, dry them very well.

Can I air fry frozen fries without thawing them?

Yes, and you should not thaw them. Frozen fries are already par-fried, so they go in straight from the freezer in one layer, with no added oil.

The short version#

Soak if you have 15 minutes, dry no matter what, use about 1 tablespoon of oil per pound, and keep the basket to one layer: 400°F / 205°C for wedges, cubes and frozen fries, 390°F / 199°C for baby potatoes and sweet potato cubes, and 380°F / 193°C for sweet potato wedges.

Always verify doneness with an instant-read thermometer. Cooking times are starting points and vary by appliance model, wattage and basket size.
